How the engine became richer—and where its costs accumulated

Frozen master 9a08b91f4; 14 snapshots, September 2–October 11, 2026. The pre-burst baseline is a September 2025 commit. Completed: 448/448 games; no failures or timeouts. Review covers 898 first-parent integration commits and 1,371 integrated commits including merged branch history.

Conclusion: substantial local optimizations are real, but the final historical default configuration is not the fastest or most CPU-efficient configuration in this workload. Preserve every new feature; matched tests support lower worker counts and quantify autosave and kernel costs; memory ownership and GPU quarantine now guide the remaining investigation.

The current frozen version averages 1,233 ticks/s, 46.90 seconds per game, 143.91 CPU-seconds per game, 2,545 µs CPU/tick, and 304.3 MiB peak RSS. The old baseline delivered 2,100 ticks/s, 26.57 seconds, 26.52 CPU-seconds, 485 µs/tick and 27.5 MiB. That is 41.3% less throughput, 76.5% longer games in wall time, 5.25× CPU per tick and 11.08× peak RSS. These versions implement different rules and workloads; the old engine is a reference, not a feature-equivalent replacement.

  1. Early September bought better behavior at a cost. Weighted path fields, topology correctness, round-trip resource routing and richer hiring replaced cheaper approximations. The early slowdown also includes a major toolchain/language/dependency modernization and historical autosaving. The historical interval cannot be assigned to one feature from these samples; a separate dispatch-only control now demonstrates a substantial chamfer cost under common historical callers.
  2. September 17–20 is the strongest efficiency success. Throughput rose 25.9% and CPU/tick fell 20.8%, with the same tick count and winner for all 32 seeds. PR #341 specializes the gradient kernel without changing field values; the new isolated kernel control also cuts CPU about 22.4% on Playground and 20.6% on Oazis, with matching outputs and final states.
  3. Late September combines smarter algorithms and parallel execution. Lazy fields, better kernels, compact hiring, pooled scratch, periodic worker pipelines and parallel AI brought 937→1,459 ticks/s. CPU/tick improved 10.7%, while elapsed time improved 40.7%; threading and shorter changed games explain why the wall-time gain is much larger than the reduction in CPU work.
  4. Early October added extensive capabilities without a net CPU/tick improvement in this sample. Terrain catalogs, ecology and preparation caches coexist with kernel improvements. October 2→5 CPU/tick stays near 973 µs while memory grows 64%. Lower whole-game time partly reflects fewer ticks, not a broad reduction in per-tick CPU cost. Manual allocation-stack review identifies about 55.4 MB of retained replay telemetry at October 5 heap peaks, absent in October 2; this is an allocation owner, not an exact decomposition of historical RSS.
  5. October 5–8 is the principal CPU regression window. Throughput falls 20.2%, CPU/tick rises 160.5%, and mean system CPU rises from 2.04 to 57.11 seconds/game. PR #961 changes the automatic native pool from four participants to the host logical CPU count. The host-sized default resolves to 32 participants inside each six-CPU affinity group. The completed eight-seed confirmation (24 full games, three configurations) shows explicit four participants with GPU enabled reduces paired wall time 26.65% and CPU 44.41% against auto32; affinity-aware auto-six reduces wall 18.16% and CPU 29.81%. All eight winner/tick outcomes match; detailed per-tick equality comes from separate 8,192-tick diagnostic runs. Broadcast wakeup microbenchmarks are mixed; their whole-game contribution remains unisolated.
  6. The final window is chiefly a memory warning. RSS rises 189→304 MiB with almost unchanged throughput and CPU/tick. PR #1045 enables GPU compute by default in headless mode; matched initialization-only controls now show about 114–116 MiB additional process RSS before dispatch. All 16 end-only unpaced GPU health runs eventually quarantined the backend; this is not proof of a driver error. Subsequent paced diagnostics keep the backend available at 1 ms/tick on Playground and 5 ms/tick on both maps; those deliberate sleeps are not benchmark improvements. Retained replay telemetry and snapshots have separately measured allocation owners; HD artwork is excluded by this headless path.

A newer same-binary Playground comparison of three versus four participants (eight paired full-game seeds) retains all winner/tick outcomes while reducing wall 4.39% and CPU/tick 12.75%. The completed Oazis pilot also favors three in its two full-game seeds, but this is limited evidence rather than an optimal count across maps.

Further controls: transitional common-torus-fixed chamfer dispatch uses about 2.57× Playground and 3.10× Oazis CPU against BFS, with reached-field and trajectory checks; the separately measured final256-policy cohort also shows high cost (2.29× Playground, 2.73× Oazis CPU). The two batches do not isolate the effect of changing the cap. Exact telemetry reservation preserves replay/GCS bytes and reduces profiled live heap, but ordinary RSS falls only about 1–2%; four-seed confirmation timing intervals cross zero. Fresh-wrapper standalone interning retains exact AIT1 and reduces model peak and retained memory, with no whole-engine gain claimed. The completed asset probe establishes cache reuse on its sample. Diagnostic GPU pacing keeps the backend healthy in tested cases; sleeps are not a speed recommendation.

The completed mature Oazis winner profile shifts the memory priority: at its single heap peak, map fields and search frontiers together own about 204.85 MB, snapshots 92.60 MB, replay telemetry 55.24 MB and team statistics/history 34.66 MB by nearest resolved caller. These are profiled allocation owners, not a decomposition of native RSS. Active versus retained capacity and published versus staged ownership need bounded diagnostics before a representation or lifetime change.

What to unwind: potentially over-large automatic worker counts, broad wakeups, eager accelerator initialization and excess speculative or retained work. What to preserve: the new routing, terrain/resource/unit/building catalogs, delayed deterministic publication, owner RNG streams, area effects, authoring support and rendering architecture. A temporary feature-off control is a diagnostic tool, not a shipping recommendation.

Completed controls: autosave off versus on in the same September 11 clone saves 31.4% wall time and CPU over 30,000 ticks; #341's exact kernel replacement gives the savings above; lower worker counts improve latest full games. Depth and periodic pipeline controls show CPU/wall tradeoffs, so less speculative work is not automatically faster. Evidence update and hypothesis ledger identify workloads, equality checks and pending tests.
