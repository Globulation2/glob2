The engine performance story, in detail

Frozen master 9a08b91f4; 14 snapshots, September 2–October 11, 2026. The pre-burst baseline is a September 2025 commit. Completed: 448/448 games; no failures or timeouts. Review covers 898 first-parent integration commits and 1,371 integrated commits including merged branch history.

Design constraint: preserve all new gameplay and engine features. The goal is a lower-cost execution core with the same behavior and capabilities. An experiment may temporarily disable a subsystem to locate its cost; that does not make removal the proposed product change.

How to read the evidence

There are four kinds of evidence here. Primary historical measurement means the 448 completed Playground games and their retained process accounting. Matched causal control means a separate same-version or source-isolated intervention with equal inputs and verified outputs for its stated workload. Historical measurement means a result reported in an original commit or PR discussion, with its workload and revision limitations. Hypothesis means a plausible mechanism whose contribution has not been isolated. Full per-tick equality belongs to separate diagnostic runs; final-state agreement in timed windows alone does not prove every intermediate state matches. Heap profiling is allocation evidence with observer overhead, not benchmark timing or uninstrumented RSS.

The audit inventories every integration interval and all 1,371 newly reachable commits, including merged branches. It screens their subjects and changed paths, then deeply reads the relevant performance diffs and PR discussions. It does not claim line-by-line review of every file in every commit. Original PR conversations are retained locally; linked historical evidence branches are identified, but every linked archive was not downloaded and independently rerun. Reported microbenchmarks are not added together as if their gains were independent.

The measured spine of the narrative

Every row is the mean of 32 complete games on the same raw 128×128 Playground map, eight Nicowars in FFA, one inert legacy local observer, and the same seed list. Four games run concurrently, each with a fixed group of three physical cores and their six logical CPUs. Every seed retains its CPU slot across revisions. All binaries were completed before the fresh cohort started; earlier pipelined measurements were archived and excluded.

TPS uses the time inside Engine::run. End-to-end time and process CPU include startup/shutdown; CPU includes all threads, user and system work. CPU/tick is the mean of per-game ratios. Peak RSS is the mean of each process's maximum resident memory. The diagnostic CPU stack chart instead divides summed CPU by summed ticks and labels that distinction. CPU affinity limits where threads run; it does not force the engine to create exactly six threads or prevent unrelated host processes using those CPUs.

Before September 3 → September 5: a large integration, not a small three-day patch

The baseline is 14b99bc82, a September 2025 commit immediately before the new master activity. The first interval integrates 492 reachable commits through 19 first-parent commits, including long-lived branch history whose author dates precede September. Calling it “three days of code” would misdescribe the size of the change.

Measured throughput halves, 2,100→1,026 TPS; CPU/tick doubles, 485→987 µs, while RSS stays near 27.5 MiB. Mean game ticks grow 17.9%. #131 modernizes build/library usage and #129 integrates extensive refactoring, correctness, AI and tooling work. Compatibility evidence in that merge does not establish performance neutrality.

The highest-value implementation suspect is the transition from queue-based BFS to repeated full-map chamfer passes: 1e228ce06 introduces it, 7ff6e3a1c makes it production, and a518d7087 removes the old BFS. The implementation repeatedly scans forward/backward until convergence; obstacles and torus direction changes can require several passes, with a later cap of 256. The completed dispatch control uses the earlier transitional 32-pass cap and passes its tested oracles. A separately labeled final256-policy pair is built with oracle/repeatability gates; all twenty-eight controls now complete, with matching fields/trajectories/repeats and four timing pairs per map: chamfer/BFS CPU 2.285× on Playground and 2.734× on Oazis, wall 2.283× and 2.730×, with equal final CRCs and essentially flat RSS. The earlier32-policy cohort gives 2.572×/3.098× CPU. These separate source-policy/run-context batches cannot establish a causal32-versus256 cap speed effect; retain them separately rather than pooling or interpreting the ratio difference as an improvement. Final September 5 also removes queue setup and consolidates templates, so cap-only adaptation does not recreate the complete final implementation. This offers a plausible source of extra cell visits. A separate common-torus-corrected transitional32 dispatch-only control now verifies chamfer against the historical BFS reference for every reached field in the chamfer process, plus byte-identical cross-binary GCS traces on both maps over 8,192 ticks. The BFS-dispatch dual wrapper compares BFS with BFS, so this is one chamfer/BFS field oracle plus cross-binary trajectory proof, not two independent mathematical oracles. Four matched timing pairs per map show chamfer using 2.57× CPU and 2.57× wall on Playground (20,000 ticks), and 3.10× CPU and 3.09× wall on Oazis (8,192 ticks), with equal final CRCs and essentially flat RSS. The source copies differ only in dispatch and share the later Nicowar torus correction. This isolates dispatch cost in those common transitional callers, not the complete final September 5 implementation or entire historical doubling. Transitional control records and final256-policy control records retain the separate evidence. Raw repeats also match detailed states but contain known historical edge-read UB; their replay files differ, so repeatability is not evidence that UB is absent.

Stable hiring tie-breaks and corrected torus queries used by Nicowar farms also change the world being simulated. More or differently located colonies can make each tick costlier. The correct investigation holds the fixed ordering and corrected map semantics, captures the same gradient inputs, and compares work counts and solver CPU. Reverting those correctness fixes would violate the preservation goal. Repairs to a Nicowar scratch leak, removal of dead logging and static type tables are likely beneficial changes in the same integration. “Modern C++ made it slower” is not a supported conclusion.

Timing scopes and AI mixes differ from this campaign.

September 8–11: richer resource routing and hiring have measurable costs

#244 / cb7e39f4e adds building × resource × swim-class round-trip fields, seeded with the return cost. Refresh limits, propagation caps and expiry bound work; saved fields preserve continuation behavior. #197 adds task and inn swaps based on combined route savings. #223 changes resource hiring to subscription-aware apportionment and prices detours.

The historical evidence needs a correction that matters to the narrative. An early comment called a 13–18% cost “swaps,” but a later controlled four-variant study separated its round-trip base: on Oazis with eleven teams, round-trip alone cost 18.2%, and round-trip plus swaps cost 15.5%. On the other fixture the figures were 13.3% and 16.4%. Thus most demonstrated cost belonged to the fields; assigning 13–18% solely to swaps would be wrong. Later read-only scoring probes did not materially improve those timings.

Those changes also produced useful behavior: a broader 48-scenario campaign reported increased unit/building growth with the stack, including a Nicowar subset with roughly 13.8% more units and 12.5% more buildings at its checkpoints. More successful colonies create more work. Preserve better routing and exchanges; optimize field refresh, scoring access and candidate scans.

Our CPU/tick rises 93.1%, throughput falls 47.4%, and RSS rises 23.2%. Historical field penalties explain a plausible part, not the entire 93% change. Earlier headless code autosaves every 256 ticks, serializing these expanding caches. Solver work, hiring work and save serialization need separate accounting. Fewer comparisons or cheaper fields must retain current decisions and saved continuation semantics.

September 11–14: correctness costs coexist with removing headless autosave work

#232 / 95c6a6071 introduces topology-generation invalidation for distant structural changes. The counter is cheap; induced field rebuilds are not. Its original Oazis study showed a 25-tick dirty throttle increasing cost about 42.6%; the merged 100-tick throttle limited the reported increase to 10.3% while eliminating stuck cases in that test. Slower refresh recovered some time but reintroduced stuck units. This is a correctness/performance tradeoff whose desired fix is better invalidation or exact repair, not stale routes.

#260 moves autosave output to a background writer while encoding still runs on the game thread; large queued snapshots retain memory. Then #289 / 9c3a4ef91 explicitly disables autosaves for headless/test-game modes. The historical-default experiment did not override this policy. Consequently the observed 14.8% CPU/tick improvement and 30% e2e reduction include avoided serialization/I/O, not just simulation kernels. System CPU falls from 3.78 to 0.10 seconds/game across this interval, consistent with much less I/O-related work.

#288 adds event/history and performance telemetry. Its collector is source-default enabled unless GLOB2_PERF_DISABLE is set, while output is gated separately. The runner does not explicitly set that disable variable; the cohort did not record every inherited environment value, so actual instrumentation activation cannot be proven solely from the command. No trustworthy sub-1% overhead claim was made. #291 and #293 change Nicowar resource protection and clearing, providing additional candidates for changed game trajectories. Cortex/Maxima-specific wins in the same period should not be credited to eight Nicowars.

Lessons for optimizing the current core without losing features

  1. Optimize the critical path and total work separately. September's pipelines improve latency more than CPU efficiency. Report throughput, CPU/tick and memory together; no single number captures both a player's responsiveness and server capacity.
  2. Make parallelism reflect available resources. A six-CPU affinity allocation is not a six-thread pool. Discover affinity/quota limits, preserve explicit controls, and benchmark useful worker counts on small and large maps. Worker memory and wakeups matter as much as compute kernels.
  3. Keep deterministic publication while changing execution placement. Ordered proposals, owner RNG streams and immutable snapshots create boundaries where implementation can improve without deleting features. Same deadlines, same consumed values and per-tick checksums are the acceptance criteria.
  4. Pay for caches and snapshots only where reused. Track logical live bytes, retained capacity, hit rate, initialization, copy volume and allocator calls independently. Pooling can cut churn while increasing RSS. Share immutable terrain/catalog data, trim unused per-worker scratch and release expired leases.
  5. Control speculation with measured demand. Deeper prefetch can reduce owner stalls but inflate total CPU. Use existing depth policies as exact-output controls, then tune deadline risk and demand prediction without changing path results.
  6. Separate product features from headless startup obligations. Preserve every renderer and GPU facility, but defer preparation until the workload actually needs it. Distinguish requested backend, initialized backend and executed jobs.
  7. Retain adverse evidence and validate the final integrated head. Several PRs honestly recorded regressions, waived final timing, or measured a precursor. Those results are research leads, not grounds to stack percentage claims. Recheck the final combined configuration with matching compiler, workload, explicit thread policy and artifacts.
  8. Measure work and feel as well as time. Include unit-ticks, building-ticks, gradient cells settled, rebuilds, AI observations, resource proposals, deaths/starvation and time-to-victory. Faster games caused by changed strategy or survival are not automatically a cheaper engine or better pacing.

Why the Oazis cohort matters

Many optimizations were designed for larger maps, and the shipped Oazis map is the user's consistent 256×256 case across the history. Its decompressed bytes have been verified identical at all fourteen snapshots. Relative to Playground, it has four times as many cells and eleven rather than eight teams; a fully populated per-cell/per-team structure could therefore scale by 5.5× before other factors. Actual live entities, geometry and cache occupancy must be measured rather than inferred from that upper-level ratio.

Larger fields may amortize queue setup, snapshot publication, worker synchronization and accelerator dispatch. Lazy exploration and reusable templates may avoid much more work. Conversely, poor invalidation, excessive speculation and retained per-worker buffers can grow more expensive. Therefore this report identifies problems observed on Playground and candidate explanations; it does not declare the larger-map design a failure. Once Oazis finishes, compare both absolute cost and each version's change within the same map, not raw Oazis time against Playground time as if they were equal workloads.

What may have been counterproductive

The strongest candidate to unwind is the assumption that automatic native parallelism should equal the whole host's logical CPU count. The next candidates are broad wakeups, eager small-workload GPU initialization, generous per-worker scratch retention and speculative depth that performs work no reader needs. Earlier increases in periodic workers and retained pool slots also need workload-sensitive validation.

These are implementation/default choices. Do not roll back terrain, catalogs, independent RNG, area effects, delayed growth, Scene separation or worker-capable gradients to match the old baseline. Historical feature removal is useful only as a temporary cost-isolation experiment. Shipping improvements must keep current capability, current rule behavior and persistence/replay/network contracts; any intentional semantic change needs the repository's simulation-version and compatibility process.
