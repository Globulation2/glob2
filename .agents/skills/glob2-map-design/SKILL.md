# From map concept to playable generator

Treat a map as an economy and a travel network that evolve during play. Its silhouette is only one part of the design. Preserve the user's concept while finding geometry, resource policy, and controls that make its intended decisions possible.

This skill is grounded in a code/comment review of all 49 built-in generators. The references distinguish current mechanisms from design recommendations; they do not certify that every existing generator is fun or balanced. Paths below are relative to this skill. Follow repository `AGENTS.md`; recheck relevant code when implementing because this guide is a source map, not a frozen engine specification.

## Turn the idea into a playable contract

Write a short design brief before coding, scaled to the request:

- What choice should players face: early pressure, contested farmland, several fronts, compact city expansion, clearing a forest, or investing in swimming? Where and why do opponents meet?
- What does every colony need to establish food, wood, population, upgrades, and an army? Budget connected building footprints, worker access, renewable crops, and exits separately.
- What is home ground, what is expansion ground, and what is worth contesting? Describe the opening, first contact, and later changes as resources spread or units gain abilities.
- What does the subject look like? Name the three or four visual signatures a person would recognise it by in a photograph, before naming any mechanism, and check the planned construction draws each one. Rice terraces built from the nearest primitive (contour rings round a summit) never read as rice terraces however they were decorated; they had to be rebuilt as long winding strips down whole hillsides, and the ring map became Hills (see "model the real thing" in [the tuning playbook](references/tuning-playbook.md)).
- Does the concept live in the landscape, or only in a starting condition? A map built around how a colony starts (a premade base, a big garrison, a head start) leaves the terrain as plumbing for that start: The Glacis, Allotments and Caravanserai started finished or staked-out bases on a lattice of identical modules, read as graph paper and circles, and gave AIs nothing to grow into (Nicowar bred 14 to 15 times a colony from its premade 48; Caravanserai starved out two colonies in three). Their rebuilds (2026-09-16) kept each theme and put it in the ground. Decide what the map is about after the opening, then design the opening to lead into it.
- What makes the subject readable is contrast, not the feature alone. A cleared glacis is invisible on a plain of grass; it reads against the woods that crowd up to it. A desert reads through dune bands, scrub hollows and rock, not through flat sand; allotments read through narrow plots each tended differently, not through identical pads. Plan the surroundings that make the signature stand out.
- Which properties are invariants, which may vary by seed, and which are player controls? State the supported sizes, aspect ratios, and colony counts. Make unsupported combinations explicit.
- Give topology words a scope: does “two crossings” mean globally, per colony, or per neighbour? Does “one entrance” remain true after swimming? Resolve consequential ambiguity before encoding the wrong route graph.
- How will the design fail in practice, and what observation would cause a revision? Examples: a crop belt engulfs homes, a tower covers the only attack route, swimming removes the intended front, or equal starts lead to radically unequal expansion.

Choose the construction family by the play contract. Recognizable shapes can use transformed masks and repeated home modules; landscapes can use fields, regions, and scored start placement; arenas can carve connected rooms and fronts from impassable ground. Hybrids are useful. Do not force an asymmetric landscape into exact symmetry merely because it is easier to validate.

## Design and implementation loop

1. **Budget geometry before decoration.** Fit homes, complete building footprints and upgrade room, sustainable crop plots, gathering edges, multiple useful routes where intended, beaches, and boundary thickness on the smallest supported map. Use toroidal distances and account for rasterization. A large grass count does not imply buildable space.
2. **Control the future resource footprint.** Separate guaranteed starter supplies, renewable farmland, ambient abundance, and structural deposits. Use water placement, dry zones and sand containment deliberately — those are the only tools. Protect roads and expansion ground. **No-growth zones are forbidden on generated maps:** the engine's saved `canResourcesGrow` tile flag is for hand-made scenarios such as the tutorial, `validateGeneratedWorld` refuses any generated world with a single no-growth tile, and the toolkit has no helper for it. A service court an inn must stand on beside its wheat is held clear with one row of sand corners, which leaves the inn beside the grain and stops any crop extending across. More wheat is not automatically better for an AI.
3. **Choose an honest fairness model.** Exact tile orbits can prove symmetry; independently rasterized wedges cannot. For asymmetric worlds, optimize actual post-settlement resource access and room, then inspect expansion and contact costs. Randomly deal sites to team indices before indexing kits or defenses. Equal starting scores are useful evidence, not proof of fair games. A home designed once and stamped by quarter turns (`turnStencilTile`/`turnStencilVertex`, `Orbits.h`) is identical tile for tile, but not for an AI whose scans run along the map's axes: with a facing per colony, The Glacis' Numbi colonies reached 60 units in one facing and 15 to 30 in the others. Draw such a facing once per map, so homes are exact translations. Record every per-colony design draw (variant, neighbour) in telemetry and check a tournament's weak starts against it.
4. **Compose shared stages.** Keep the layout in a reconstructible `design(request, context)`, stamp terrain, settle colonies, furnish resources, repair only permitted failures, and validate the finished world. Inspect `shared/` before inventing a helper; extract a reusable routine on its second use. Fix primitive bugs with regressions and account for every affected generator's output.
5. **Make variation strategic.** Change routes, field locations, district/cell shapes, crossings or objectives within the concept's invariants, using named seed streams. Mere resource sprite changes or shuffling team labels are insufficient replay variety. For exact symmetry, vary a fundamental domain or an invariant field and reproduce its orbits. **Variety is a basic rule of map making, in appearance as well as play:** a map whose every cell, field or module is the same stamp reads as monotonous even when it plays well. Give repeated elements several designs and patterns (Canals' varied blocks are the model), break up large uniform areas with small terrain features, and check a preview for sameness as well as for ugliness; keep the variation inside the concept's look ("Maps can't be ugly") and its fairness model. **A hard-coded shape comes in several designs.** When a generator stamps a specific drawn shape — a home module, a fort, a town plot, a garden, a pond — build a few designs that meet the same contract (and turns and mirrors where the concept allows), choose one per map from its own named stream, and give every colony that same design so the opening stays fair; neutral copies may draw their own. The same stamp on every map is too boring, however good it is. Canals' block kinds, City states' home kinds and commons hearts, Hedgerow Country's and Breachable Highlands' centrepieces, and the home designs of Forts, Lava shield, Sierpiński Gardens and Hilbert River are the models. Pin each design and compare it with the previous release across sizes and colony counts before shipping: the weakest design sets the generator's success rate ([shaped generators](references/shaped-generators.md#variety-without-dissolving-the-concept) has the method and the traps).
6. **Expose meaningful variants.** Controls should change a player decision, such as crossing width or room allocation. Wire every ambient resource layer to its applicable registered resource amount. Document guaranteed supplies and structural-wall exceptions. Test both scarcity and crowding: 0% may retain the starter guarantee, while high amounts must leave room and routes.
7. **Instrument and analyze internal decisions.** Use the [telemetry guide](../../../docs/map-generators/TELEMETRY.md) to record effective variants, requested/placed features, calibration inputs/outputs and actual fallback or omission branches. Reuse existing values and cheap counters; collection stays off outside requested JSON output, and telemetry must add no RNG draws, grid scans or expensive analysis. Keep stable, bounded metrics that explain many seeds; remove temporary per-tile/per-candidate debug traces. Bulk-generate a seed/settings matrix and join internal observations with final-map metrics, inspecting failures, missing records and rare variants before tuning. Benchmark generation with collection on and off.
8. **Verify progressively and revise.** First check structural invariants and final geometry, then seed/parameter sweeps and preview comparisons, then real AI games and human play. Look at the preview as a maintainer will, before any number: does it read as the concept at a glance, with nothing invented where it claims reality and nothing drawn with compasses where it claims nature (the [playbook](references/tuning-playbook.md) lists the remarks first looks produce and the cheap answers)? Before calling a generator done, play a rotation tournament on it and read the per-start economy before the win counts: the [tuning playbook](references/tuning-playbook.md) names the shapes a result takes (a doomed start, a food-capped economy, a peaceful stalemate, an AI-specific collapse) and the remedy each calls for. Before the tournament, calibrate cheaply: a 20,000- to 30,000-tick four-AI game on your map beside the same game on a reference generator (Forts, Hedgerow Country) with the same seed shows in minutes whether the economy is food-capped, and the AI's own telemetry says why (the [playbook](references/tuning-playbook.md#calibrate-locally-before-a-tournament)). Play every AI players are offered; each fails differently. Test a remedy through an existing control before writing code, and pin the affected variant so paired runs compare the same kind of map. Include late growth, expansion and alternate movement abilities. Tune on one seed set and check another; retain failing seeds. Revisit the design when a repair erases its identity: **maps can't be ugly**, and a change made for mobility or playability that destroys the map's aesthetic vision is not finished — find the version that keeps the picture (the [playbook](references/tuning-playbook.md#the-picture-is-the-first-review) records the cases). Look at a finished game, not only a preview: growth, overgrowth and shorelines laid after the beach pass only show up there.

## Completion evidence

For generator work, report the intended play experience, changed controls/primitives, supported parameter envelope, reproducible commands and artifact locations, and observed limits. Include maps with request JSON, previews, relevant sweep results, internal telemetry and its bulk analysis, generation timing comparisons, and game evidence proportional to the change. Distinguish generation repeatability, static fairness, AI performance and human fun. Follow the repository's compatibility and review rules for changes extending into simulation or existing gameplay; do not claim unperformed platform comparisons or playtests.
